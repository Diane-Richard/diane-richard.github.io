Water and soil quality monitoring through participatory science in Madagascar

Training students and teachers to monitor the biodiversity, water and soils around their schools, with low-cost tools checked by AI.

Waterborne disease is a leading cause of child mortality in Madagascar, yet systematic water-quality monitoring exists only in the capital. I lead the water and soil part of a participatory-science program run with local institutions. High-school students and teachers, trained through the École Normale Supérieure and Centre ValBio, collect and catalog the plants and insects around their schools with frugal tools, from Foldscope microscopes to DNA barcoding at the field station. They’re now learning to measure water and soil quality too, with low-cost kits, and we’re building AI and satellite tools to check and extend these measurements. The approach is One Health: we read the health of ecosystems and the health of people together, so that health partners and decision-makers can act on the data.

  • Shaped the study’s water-and-health question and its AI-validation design.
  • Building the Field Companion, an offline AI assistant that guides students and teachers through sampling.
  • Taught science lessons and ran water and soil sampling with high-school students in Madagascar (2026).
